How does the camera head read an image?

On the camera member of the stack: what the image depicts and how it was captured, in one row, read by one booster.

The camera head gives the detector its take on whether a camera made the image. It reads two kinds of evidence at once: what the image depicts, from a frozen DINOv2 embedding, and whether a sensor made its pixels, from the capture signature. Until 2026-10-21 it was called the fusion head.

It is one member of the stack, with its own score beside every other member's. The combiner revision anchors on that score unless the screenshot gate fired. Chapter 2 shows the specialist members, and chapter 3 the stages and the revisions.

One row of 393 numbers

Every image becomes one row. The first numbers are DINOv2-small's embedding of the whole image, its short edge resized to 256 and its centre cropped to 224. The last nine are the capture signature, measured on the native pixels.

The order is the booster's and the store's, and it never changes. A model trained on one order and run on another would read the capture peak where it expects an embedding value. The head the bundle ships reads 393 columns.

One row since 2026-10-21

Two wider rows were trained and measured on 2026-10-21: both DINOv2 readings with capture, 777 columns, and those with the warp residual's 38 statistics, 815. Neither passed the bar fixed before it ran, and both left the code the same day with the restructure. Chapter 10 keeps their numbers.

What the image depicts

DINOv2 was trained to describe images, not to judge them. Its embedding places a portrait near other portraits and a watercolour near other watercolours. That is an axis no measurement of a camera can see: a generator's typical subjects and styles.

The embedding alone is no detector. The research's v2.0 probe, quoted here, found every semantic and physical feature tied to the data it was trained on. No feature held across generators it had not seen. The fusion works because each axis covers the other's blind spot.

Whether a sensor made the pixels

The capture signature is measured, not learned. A camera's sensor and its demosaicing leave a periodic trace in the highest frequencies of the native pixels. A generator's output does not carry it, and a resize destroys it.

The lens reads a centre crop at native resolution, takes its high-pass residual and finds the peak of that residual's spectrum. A peak of 30 or more counts as present. The short side must reach 512 pixels, or the image is marked invalid, because the peak is not reliable below that size.

All nine values enter the row as they are. The capture member reports the same measurement in the stack, and when the signature is present the combiner revision silences every generated-side member, which chapter 2 shows.

The booster

The camera head is an ensemble of 500 trees of depth 5 over the row. VeilGarde trained it from scratch on its own feature store, and chapter 5 shows that fit.

Its probability is called at 0.5 with an open band of 0.04 either side. A score inside the band is inconclusive, above it generated, below it real.

The camera member reports its score on every image, screenshots included. When the flat-UI gate fires the combiner anchors on the screenshot member instead: a screenshot is real but not a capture, and a head trained on camera rows would read it as generated.

What the warp no longer adds

Until the two lanes, the row held four more columns: the warp lens's score and three sharpness values. The research refit the head from scratch without each input and scored it on store rows it had not trained on.

In the combiner revision

An image whose capture signature is present carries the sensor's own evidence: the combiner lets the camera member's score stand and silences the generated-side members. An image without one is where the camera head matters most.

For those images the combiner pools the camera member, its anchor, with the art, blur and human members, and the anchor enters with the largest weight, 1.6641.

Its logit is capped at 4.0, an evidence member's at 2.0, so a saturated member cannot overturn a confident anchor. The next chapter shows that arithmetic.

How the camera head moves

A booster trained from scratch on new data does not reproduce the old one's boundary, so it can lose cases the old one got right. The research learned this five times over: every from-scratch store retrain failed the battery, and warm starts passed.

So the flywheel moves the camera head only by a warm start. It keeps the live head's trees byte for byte and adds new ones fitted to what they still get wrong, 80 trees at a learning rate of 0.03 by default. Promote refuses a camera head that does not keep the live head's trees.

The shipped head is the exception, and the reason is its width. The head before it read 397 columns, and a warm start cannot continue a model on columns it never had. So it was trained from scratch, with an evaluation twin to measure it on rows it never saw.

Under the warp era the battery decided what a warm start may add. Measured on the warp era's head, a start of 80 trees flipped IMG_0291, the knife-edge flux anchor, to real. The promote was refused, and a start of 20 trees kept it generated and passed.

The new head calls IMG_0291 real. It is one of the four images the battery's version 1 sets aside for research, measured and not gated, until a candidate calls them right.
